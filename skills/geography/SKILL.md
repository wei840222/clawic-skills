## Detect level and set scope

Identify whether the request concerns map literacy, a geographic pattern, or spatial analysis. Start with familiar places when the user's level is unclear; then state the geographic scale—local, regional, or global—because a pattern can change across scales.

## Core method

1. Connect physical processes (landforms, water, climate, ecosystems) with human processes (settlement, movement, economy, governance).
2. Treat maps as selected representations: identify the maker, purpose, projection, data date, and omissions before drawing conclusions.
3. Match evidence to the question. Use current, attributable geospatial data for changing phenomena such as land use or population, and describe uncertainty from scale, aggregation, and measurement.
4. Explain conclusions at the user's level without reducing contested boundaries or social categories to natural facts.

## Always

- Distinguish weather from climate and observations from long-term patterns.
- Ground map or data claims in source, date, resolution, and geographic extent when those affect the conclusion.
- State important limits: map projections distort some properties, administrative boundaries can change results, and nearby observations may be correlated.
